How are chromatograms calculated?

The simulator calculates mobile-phase properties and column efficiency, determines when each compound elutes and how broad its peak is, then adds the individual concentration profiles and detector noise. The subsections follow that calculation sequence. Equations use consistent units unless specific numerical units are stated. Throughout the notes, t is elapsed time from injection; Δ indicates a change or increment, π is the circle constant, √ denotes a square root, ln is the natural logarithm, log10 is the base-10 logarithm, and exp is the exponential function. The function min selects the smallest of its arguments.

Adapted from the supporting information for Boswell et al., Journal of Chemical Education (2013). It is intended for teaching relationships between conditions and separations, rather than predicting an experimental chromatogram precisely.

Column porosity, hold-up volume, and flow velocity

The column contains mobile phase both between the stationary-phase particles and inside their accessible pores. Interparticle porosity, εe, is the fraction of the empty-column volume between particles. Intraparticle porosity, εi, is the fraction of each particle’s volume accessible to mobile phase. Total porosity, εT, is the fraction of the column volume accessible to mobile phase:

εT = εe + εi(1 − εe)

For a column with internal diameter d and length L, the cross-sectional area O, mobile-phase hold-up volume V0, and hold-up time t0 are:

O = πd2/4
V0 = O L εT
t0 = V0/F

F is volumetric flow rate. Hold-up time is the time required for an unretained solute to pass through the column; any post-column tubing delay is calculated separately. Use consistent volume and time units when evaluating these equations.

The same flow rate can be expressed as several linear velocities:

us = F/O
ue = us/εe
u = us/εT = L/t0

Superficial velocity, us, treats the column as an empty tube. Interstitial velocity, ue, refers to the space between particles. Chromatographic velocity, u, accounts for all accessible mobile-phase volume and describes an unretained solute.

Eluent viscosity

Viscosity, η, determines resistance to flow and influences solute diffusion. The simulator uses empirical expressions for acetonitrile–water and methanol–water mixtures. Let φ be the volume fraction of solvent B, from 0 to 1, and let TK = T + 273.15, where T is temperature in °C and TK is absolute temperature in kelvin. Both expressions return viscosity in cP.

Acetonitrile–water

ln(η) = φ(−3.476 + 726/TK)
+ (1 − φ)(−5.414 + 1566/TK)
+ φ(1 − φ)(−1.762 + 929/TK)

Methanol–water

ln(η) = φ(−4.597 + 1211/TK)
+ (1 − φ)(−5.961 + 1736/TK)
+ φ(1 − φ)(−6.215 + 2809/TK)

Viscosity is the exponential of the corresponding expression. These empirical mixture relationships account for the nonlinear change in viscosity with composition; viscosity is not simply a volume-weighted average of the pure solvents.

For gradient runs, the fixed efficiency and tubing-dispersion calculations use the initial composition. The viscosity and backpressure overlays instead follow the changing composition at the column inlet.

Backpressure

The packed-column pressure drop, ΔPcol, combines Darcy’s law with a Kozeny–Carman permeability approximation:

ΔPcol = 180η L us(1 − εe)2 / (εe3dp2)

dp is particle diameter. In SI units, viscosity is in Pa·s, lengths in meters, and velocity in m/s; the result is in pascals. The simulator converts this to bar (1 bar = 100,000 Pa). At fixed flow and column dimensions, smaller particles increase pressure approximately as 1/dp2.

Post-column tubing contributes a Hagen–Poiseuille pressure drop:

ΔPtube = 8η Ltube F/(πr4)
ΔPtotal = ΔPcol + ΔPtube

Here ΔPtube is the tubing pressure drop, ΔPtotal is total backpressure, Ltube is tubing length, and r is the tubing’s internal radius. The symbols η, L, us, εe, and F retain their definitions above: viscosity, column length, superficial velocity, interparticle porosity, and volumetric flow rate. The strong fourth-power dependence explains why narrow tubing can add substantial resistance. The calculation assumes laminar flow and does not include separate pressure losses from fittings or a detector flow cell. For gradients, the pressure overlay scales the initial pressure by the ratio of current inlet viscosity to initial viscosity; it does not integrate a spatial viscosity profile along the column.

Diffusion and column efficiency

The simulator estimates a representative analyte diffusion coefficient with a Wilke–Chang relationship:

De = 7.4 × 10−8TK√(χ M) / (η Vm0.6)

De is the representative analyte diffusion coefficient in cm²/s, η is in cP, M is the solvent molecular weight in g/mol, and Vm is the analyte molar volume in cm³/mol. The current implementation first averages the molar volumes of the selected compounds, without concentration weighting, then uses that average to obtain one diffusion coefficient for the whole sample.

χ = 2.6(1 − φ) + 1.9φ
M = 18(1 − φ) + Morg φ

The solvent association parameter χ interpolates between water and the organic modifier. The organic modifier’s molecular weight, Morg, is 41 for acetonitrile and 32 for methanol. Compound molar volumes are supplied in the library or entered for custom compounds.

Reduced velocity, ν, is dimensionless and compares convective motion with molecular diffusion:

ν = ue dp/De

Here ue, dp, and De must use consistent length units. Reduced plate height follows the reduced van Deemter equation:

h = A + B/ν + C ν
H = h dp
N = L/H

A, B, and C describe eddy dispersion, longitudinal diffusion, and resistance to mass transfer. h is the dimensionless reduced plate height; H is the height equivalent to a theoretical plate (HETP); N is the theoretical plate count. A smaller plate height gives more plates and narrower calculated peaks. All compounds share this efficiency estimate, which uses the initial composition in gradient mode.

Isocratic retention factors and retention times

The dimensionless retention factor, k′, is the ratio of time a compound spends retained by the stationary phase to time it spends in the mobile phase. For each compound, the simulator approximates solvent-strength dependence with a linear relationship between log retention factor and organic fraction:

log10(k′) = log10(k′w) − S φ

k′w is the extrapolated retention factor in water, and S describes sensitivity to solvent composition. Temperature dependence is represented by linear fits. In the notation used by the custom-compound controls:

log10(k′w) = mw T + bw
−S = mφ T + bφ
log10(k′) = mw T + bw + (mφ T + bφ)φ

T is in °C here, not kelvin. The coefficients mw and bw are the slope and intercept of log10(k′w) versus temperature. The coefficients mφ and bφ are the slope and intercept of −S versus temperature. The slopes have units of °C⁻¹; the intercepts are dimensionless. All four depend on compound, stationary phase, and organic solvent. The last two coefficients describe −S, so they are added in the final expression. These empirical fits provide an educational approximation; extrapolation across wide composition or temperature ranges is not a guarantee of experimental retention.

tR,col = t0(1 + k′)
tR = tR,col + ttube

Here tR,col is the time from injection to the column outlet, ttube is the post-column tubing transit time, and tR is the retention time at the detector. Increasing k′ makes retention longer. The selected-compound position overlay follows z = L min[1, t/(t0(1 + k′))]; where z is distance from the column inlet and t is elapsed time. The position reaches the column outlet before any post-column delay.

Gradient formation and dwell volume

The programmed gradient is interpolated linearly between the time/composition rows. Before time zero the first composition is used; after the final row the last composition is held. The composition arriving at the column differs from this program because of non-mixing and mixing volumes upstream.

tdelay = Vnonmix/F
φin(t) = φprogram(t − tdelay)

Here Vnonmix is the upstream non-mixing volume, tdelay is its transport delay, φprogram(t) is the programmed solvent-B fraction, and φin(t) is the delayed fraction entering the mixer. Mixing volume behaves as a well-mixed reservoir, initially filled with the starting composition. For a short time increment Δt, a volume FΔt enters and leaves the mixer. Its composition is updated by a volume balance:

φmix,new = φmix,old + α(φin − φmix,old)
α = min[1, FΔt/Vmix]

The mixing-chamber volume is Vmix. The fractions φmix,old and φmix,new describe the chamber before and after a time step. The dimensionless replacement fraction α is the pumped volume divided by mixer volume, capped at 1. The resulting fraction φmix(t) is the column-inlet composition as a function of time.

If mixing volume is zero, the delayed incoming composition is used directly. The update is also limited to complete replacement when a time step pumps at least one mixer volume. Mixing rounds and delays changes in composition, while non-mixing volume simply shifts the program in time. Their sum is the nominal dwell volume.

The calculation horizon is the time span used for numerical integration of the gradient and compound migration. The numerical mixer time step is this horizon divided by 999. This integration grid is separate from the detector sampling rate. The solvent-B overlay shows the resulting composition at the column inlet.

Gradient migration and retention times

During a gradient, a compound’s retention factor changes as the solvent surrounding it changes. The simulator divides migration into small steps and recalculates k′ using the same composition and temperature relationship as in isocratic mode.

A useful way to express this calculation is in the solvent-time coordinate θ: elapsed time minus the unretained travel time to the compound’s position. For a uniform column, the elution condition is:

∫0tR,col − t0 dθ / [t0 k′(φmix(θ), T)] = 1

At each step the model evaluates the inlet composition at the compound’s solvent-time coordinate. With x = z/L as fractional column position (0 at the inlet and 1 at the outlet), Δx as its increment, Δt as the elapsed-time increment, and Δθ as the solvent-time integration step, migration is accumulated as:

Δx = Δθ/(t0 k′)
Δt = Δθ + t0Δx

The simulation tracks elapsed time, retention factor, and position until x reaches 1. It interpolates across the exit step to estimate the column retention time, then adds post-column tubing delay. The step Δθ is the calculation horizon divided by 1000. In automatic-duration mode, the horizon is extended as needed, up to the acquisition limit. A compound that has not exited within the available horizon is reported as not eluted.

The selected-compound retention-factor and position overlays are interpolated from this migration history. The position is reported in millimeters along the column.

Post-column tubing: delay and dispersion

A tube of length Ltube and internal radius r adds a volume and transport time:

Vtube = πr2Ltube
ttube = Vtube/F
utube = F/(πr2)

Here Vtube is internal tubing volume, ttube is tubing transit time, and utube is mean flow velocity in the tube.

The model estimates longitudinal spreading from molecular diffusion and the parabolic velocity profile in an open tube. It converts spatial variance to time variance:

σz2 = 2De Ltube/utube + r2Ltube utube/(24De)
σtube2 = σz2/utube2

The quantity σz is the standard deviation of axial position caused by tubing dispersion, and σtube is the corresponding standard deviation in arrival time. Their squares are spatial variance (length²) and time variance (time²), respectively. Use consistent length and time units. This is a simplified laminar-tube dispersion estimate, rather than a detailed model of fittings, bends, or a detector cell. Tubing pressure is included separately in total backpressure. With zero tubing length, the tubing delay, dispersion, and pressure contributions are all zero.

Peak widths in isocratic and gradient elution

Each compound is represented by a Gaussian peak with time standard deviation σ. Independent broadening contributions are added as variances. In isocratic mode:

σ2 = tR2/N + τ2 + σtube2 + (Vinj/F)2/12

The terms represent column-efficiency broadening, detector response, tubing dispersion, and a rectangular injection pulse. τ is the detector time constant, and Vinj is injection volume; Vinj/F is injection duration. The current implementation uses detected retention time, including tubing delay, in the first term. The detector is approximated by an added variance, not by a full electronic-filter response.

In gradient mode, the width depends on retention factor near the column exit, k′e:

σ2 = [t0(1 + k′e)]2/N + τ2 + σtube2

This approximation can produce much narrower peaks than using total gradient retention time in the column term. Injection-volume broadening is omitted in gradient mode. This approximates focusing for compounds well retained at the start, but can underestimate broadening for poorly retained compounds. No separate gradient band-compression correction is applied.

The table reports σ in seconds. For a Gaussian peak, full width at half maximum is approximately 2.355σ; the conventional baseline width is approximately 4σ. The model produces symmetric peaks and does not simulate tailing, fronting, or column overload.

Detector response and chromatogram construction

The simulator assumes an ideal concentration-sensitive detector with the same molar response for every compound. Real detectors generally respond differently to different compounds: a UV detector gives a stronger signal for compounds that absorb more strongly at the selected wavelength, while a mass spectrometer’s response depends on ionization and detection efficiency. Readily ionized or already ionic compounds can give stronger responses, depending on measurement conditions.

For compound i, injected amount ni is sample concentration csample,i multiplied by injection volume Vinj. The subscript i identifies a compound in the sample:

ni = csample,i Vinj

Each compound contributes a Gaussian concentration profile:

ci(t) = ni/[F σi√(2π)]
× exp[−(t − tR,i)2/(2σi2)]

Here ci(t) is compound i’s concentration at the detector at time t, tR,i is its detected retention time, and σi is its peak’s time standard deviation. With amount in moles and flow in liters per second, this gives concentration in mol/L. The display converts to an appropriate concentration unit. Integrated peak area is ni/F; for a fixed amount and flow, a narrower peak is taller. Overlapping peaks add together.

ctotal(t) = ∑i ci(t) + coffset + εnoise(t)

Here ctotal(t) is the total detector signal expressed as concentration, ∑i means summation over all sample compounds, coffset is the constant baseline offset, and εnoise(t) is the zero-mean random detector-noise contribution at time t. The offset shifts the entire baseline. A new Gaussian-noise realization is generated whenever the chromatogram is recalculated. In the implementation’s numerical units, the noise standard deviation in µM is the Noise control value divided by 1000√(τ), with τ entered in seconds. Thus, increasing the detector time constant broadens peaks and reduces noise. Zooming, panning, and display-only changes reuse the stored samples and do not generate new noise.

Resolution, run duration, and detector sampling

Resolution compares the separation in retention time with the widths of a pair of peaks:

Rs = |tR,2 − tR,1| / [2(σ1 + σ2)]

Here Rs is dimensionless resolution; subscripts 1 and 2 identify the two compounds being compared. Their detected retention times are tR,1 and tR,2, and their peak standard deviations are σ1 and σ2. Vertical bars denote absolute value.

For each eluted compound, the results table compares it with the preceding and following compounds in retention order and reports the smaller resolution. This is the more difficult adjacent separation, which need not be the smallest retention-time gap when widths differ. With no neighboring eluted compound, resolution is undefined. Minimum resolution below the graph is the smallest adjacent-pair value among compounds whose retention times lie within the acquired run.

Automatic run duration uses the last-eluting compound:

tend,requested = 1.05(tR,last + 3σlast)

Here tend,requested is the requested acquisition end time, tR,last is the last-eluting compound’s detected retention time, and σlast is its peak standard deviation. The three-standard-deviation allowance includes most of the final peak’s trailing tail; the additional 5% leaves space afterward. Manual duration can instead be set in Time range & sampling.

At sampling rate fs in samples per second, detector samples are taken at tj = j/fs. Here j is the integer sample index (0, 1, 2, …), and tj is the time of that sample in seconds. The endpoint is rounded upward to a sampling interval. A maximum of 100,000 points is allowed, giving a maximum acquired time of 99,999/fs seconds. If the requested run exceeds that limit, acquisition stops early and a warning is shown. Sampling covers the full acquired chromatogram; zooming changes only the viewed window.

Custom compounds and random unknowns

Custom compounds use the same retention equations as library compounds. Enter a molar volume and four retention coefficients for each organic solvent. Molar volume affects the sample’s representative diffusion coefficient; the coefficients determine retention as composition and temperature change.

Random unknowns are synthetic teaching compounds, not measured chemical identities. Their retention coefficients are generated from correlated distributions, with random variation, and separate parameter sets for acetonitrile and methanol. Molar volume is drawn between 90 and 190 cm³/mol, and concentration is drawn logarithmically between 1 and 100 µM. Generated numerical parameters are rounded to three significant figures. Values explicitly entered for a custom compound retain the supplied precision.
